=== Hearth Venting Planner ===
Status: Active / current (Live · in development), 2026
Project page: https://www.davidcreason.com/projects/chimney-planner/

Summary: Enter your home's measurements, pick a manufacturer and venting system (DuraVent DuraTech, ICC EXCEL), and get a complete parts list that follows the manufacturer's installation rules and NFPA 211.

Planning a wood-stove or fireplace chimney means cross-referencing a thick manufacturer catalog, clearance rules, and NFPA 211. This planner does it for you: pick a manufacturer and venting system, describe the installation, and it returns every part you need, with quantities and part numbers.

What it does

- Lets you choose a manufacturer and venting system (currently DuraVent DuraTech and ICC EXCEL, 5″–8″) and switches rules and parts automatically while keeping your measurements.

- Walks you through the appliance, ceiling, attic, and roof measurements, or loads a worked example to start from.

- Applies the strictest rule among the manufacturer’s installation manual, NFPA 211, and your appliance manual’s own limits.

- Builds the parts list by location (ceiling, chimney pipe, attic, roof, top), flags anything to double-check, and exports to CSV or a priced dealer order.

Why it matters

- Turns hours of cross-checking a catalog, a code book, and an appliance manual into a few minutes of measurements.

- Never shorts a requirement: heights and clearances can be exceeded but never undercut, and pipe lengths always round up.

- Catches problems before anything is bought, like an offset that won’t fit in the attic or a chase too wide for stock flashing.

- Gets the order right the first time, with exact part numbers, quantities, and optional retailer/distributor SKU and price mapping.

- Works for any stove or fireplace: you enter what its manual requires, so the planner never falls behind new models.

Built for

- Homeowners planning an install and wanting to know what it takes before they start.

- Installers and sweeps quoting jobs quickly and consistently.

- Hearth retailers and distributors building a complete, priced order with their own SKUs at the counter.

Coming next

- Offsets on through-the-wall installations

- Free-standing stove placement and stovepipe

- Additional residential chimney, pellet, and direct-vent product lines

- AI that reads an appliance manual and fills in its requirements

A planning aid, not an installation approval. Follow local code and have the finished system inspected by a certified professional.

=== ScopeOut: Guided Deal Scoping ===
Status: Active / current (Live demo), 2026
Project page: https://www.davidcreason.com/projects/scopeout/

Tags: Sales engineering, Rules engine, Catalog-driven, Approval workflow, Vercel serverless, Vanilla JS
Summary: A rules-based configurator that turns discovery answers into a validated software deal scope. Editable rules, price books, discount approvals, and branded buyer summaries, all driven by one catalog file.

Scoping a software deal usually means a rep applying packaging rules from memory or a spreadsheet, and the mistakes show up later as a surprise re-scope, a discount fight, or an unhappy first renewal. I built ScopeOut to turn those rules into a guided discovery flow, so every rep scopes the same way and every decision shows its reason.

The problem

- Packaging rules live in a rep’s head or a pricing spreadsheet, so two reps can scope the same customer two different ways.

- Discovery answers rarely make it into the CRM in a usable form, so the reasoning behind a deal is lost by the time it reaches deal desk or delivery.

- Changing packaging means retraining every rep, and old rules linger for months.

The approach

- Discovery first: the rep answers plain-language questions from the call, and the scope builds itself as they go.

- Rules, not guesses: rules can only raise the requirement, so the strictest one sets the plan, and every decision is shown with its reason.

- One catalog file per product: questions, plans, rules, price books and outputs all live in data. Import a new file and the tool reconfigures with no code changes.

- Built on a proven engine: the same rules engine runs my chimney planner , which shows it isn’t tied to one industry.

For the sales team

- Example scenarios load a realistic customer in one click, for training or for a live call.

- A bill of scope, a plain-language summary, and a CRM-ready record come out of every session.

- Discounts above a rep’s limit need a manager’s approval, and buyers never see a price that hasn’t been approved.

- Buyers can get a branded email summary, sent by a small serverless function protected by a CAPTCHA and rate limits.

For RevOps

- Packaging, price books, discount limits and simple rules are edited in a draft, then published to every rep at once.

- A change log shows exactly what moved between versions, price by price.

- Catalog imports are validated line by line, so a broken rule or a typo in a formula is caught before it reaches a rep.

At a glance

- 1 catalog file per product: questions, plans, rules and prices

- 2 industries on one engine: software deals and chimney systems

- 3 roles: rep, sales manager, RevOps admin

Using it on a sales call

- Start with discovery, not a feature tour, and let the prospect’s answers decide what the scope needs.

- Load a scenario that matches their situation, so they watch their own deal get scoped.

- Switch to the buyer view to show what their own customers would see.

- Send a branded summary at the end, so the scope and its reasoning travel with the deal.

What I’d highlight

- Solutions engineering end to end. Defining the buyer’s problem, designing the rules model, and building the tool that solves it.

- Configuration as data. Moving business rules out of code means the people who own the rules can change them.

- AI-assisted development. Built quickly with an AI coding assistant, while I owned the product decisions, the rules, and the testing.

Try it yourself

- Switch to Buyer to see the self-serve versions for executives and project leads.

- On the Admin tab, choose the RevOps admin role, change a rule, and publish it.

- Import a catalog on the Admin tab: try the clean catalog , then the one with planted errors to see validation catch them.

=== Vendor Price Sheet → Jobber Import ETL ===
Status: Active / current (Live · in development), 2019–2026
Project page: https://www.davidcreason.com/projects/jobber-import-etl/
Links: Launch the demo: https://project-creason.github.io/jobber_import_etl/; Source on GitHub: https://github.com/project-creason/jobber_import_etl
Tags: Python, pandas, ETL, Data quality, Jobber
Summary: A config-driven Python pipeline that turns any manufacturer's price sheet into a clean, validated Jobber product import, with every rejected row explained.

Crestwood Home & Hearth sold and installed products from many manufacturers, and Jobber, its field-service software, needed every one of them in its product list. Each manufacturer publishes its price sheet in its own layout, so each Jobber import file was built one vendor at a time. This pipeline replaces that with one repeatable run.

The problem

- No two price sheets match. One manufacturer's sheet has part number, list, net, UPC, weight, and box dimensions; another has a model number and a single price.

- Jobber accepts exactly five columns: name, description, price, taxable, and category.

- The sheets carry small data problems that break an import or show up on a customer's quote: broken characters, inch marks, prices stored as text, placeholder rows, and duplicates.

The approach

- Extract: reads Excel or CSV, finds the header row under any title banner, detects the text encoding, and picks the right sheet.

- Transform: maps each vendor's columns to Jobber's five, adds the vendor prefix to the SKU, parses prices, and cleans the text.

- Validate: rows with no SKU, no price, or a $0 price, and duplicate SKUs, go to a rejects file with the reason.

- Load: writes the Jobber CSV, the rejects file, and a run report.

What the cleanup actually fixes

Every example below is a real row from a manufacturer's catalog.

| | Problem | In the price sheet | In the Jobber import

| Prices stored as text | List: "6,022.00" | Cost: 6022
| SKU needs a vendor prefix | 017-01-H-SM-RBC | AFD 017-01-H-SM-RBC
| Two apostrophes used as an inch mark | 9.69’’ (246MM) CEILING MOUNT POLE | 9.69" (246MM) CEILING MOUNT POLE
| Hidden control character from a Windows export | 8"␝ x 5.5"␝ | 8" x 5.5"
| Escaped symbols | COVER,AFD \#435 BRDX LARGE | COVER,AFD #435 BRDX LARGE
| $0 placeholder row | STAND - TUNGSTEN PORTABLE - BOX 2 OF 2, 0 | Rejected: zero price

Design choices

- Configuration, not code. Each vendor is a short entry in a JSON file: which file names it matches, its SKU prefix, and which columns hold the SKU, description, and price. Adding a manufacturer takes a minute.

- Nothing disappears silently. Every dropped row lands in a rejects file with the reason, so a person can fix the source instead of guessing.

- Safe to re-run. Finished Jobber files are skipped, and SKUs that already carry a prefix don't get a second one.

- Pricing is a setting. Use the list price as is, or start from dealer cost with a markup.

Results so far

- Tested on real rows from three manufacturers, chosen because they contain the problems above.

- 15 rows imported cleanly; 3 rejected, each with the correct reason: a missing price, a $0 item, and a duplicate part number.

Try it, and what's next

- Try the live demo : run the sample price sheets or your own, right in the browser. It runs the same Python code as the repo, and files never leave your computer.

- Run it on every full price sheet in the catalog.

- Watch a shared Drive folder and convert new price sheets automatically when they arrive.

=== Property ETL Bench ===
Status: Active / current (In use · built for a REIT), 2026
Project page: https://www.davidcreason.com/projects/property-etl-bench/
Links: Read the case study: https://www.davidcreason.com/projects/property-etl-bench/
Tags: JavaScript, HTML, CSS, ETL, Yardi Voyager, Batch processing, AI-assisted development
Summary: A browser-based ETL tool that turns raw property spreadsheets into Yardi Voyager import files, setting up about 30 properties in a few minutes instead of 5+ hours by hand.

Every time a property joined the portfolio, its details arrived as a spreadsheet that someone reshaped by hand into the import files Yardi Voyager expects. I built a browser tool that does the whole batch in one pass.

The problem

- New property details arrived as spreadsheets, but Yardi Voyager only accepts its own import layouts.

- Each property took 10 to 15 minutes of repetitive, careful reshaping by hand.

- A mistyped code or a missed column usually didn't surface until the import failed, or until bad data was already in the system.

The approach

- Parse & normalize: reads the spreadsheet, standardizes headers, and trims and types every value.

- Validate & map: checks required fields and formats, then maps source columns to the target import layout. Problems are flagged inline before anything is generated.

- Generate: writes the Yardi import CSVs for every property in the batch at once.

- Review & export: the user previews every output before downloading, so a person stays in the loop.

Results

- 10–15 min per property, done by hand before

- ~30 properties set up in the most recent run

- Minutes for the entire batch, versus 5+ hours by hand

Catching bad data at the validation step also means import errors get fixed in seconds by the person who has the context, instead of being discovered later in Yardi.

Built to extend

- Parsing and validation are separate from output generation, so supporting another target system means adding a new mapping and output step. The front half stays the same.

- The pattern fits any organization that loads records from spreadsheets into one or more systems of record, well beyond real estate.

- Plain JavaScript, HTML, and CSS in a single page: no server to stand up or secure, and the data never leaves the user's computer.

What I'd highlight

- Business analysis first. The hard part was understanding the target system's import rules well enough to encode them, not writing the transformation.

- AI-assisted development. I built it with an AI coding assistant, moving quickly from requirements to working software while I owned the design, the data rules, and the testing.

- Designed for the operator. Inline validation and a review step make it safe for someone who isn't an engineer to run.

This tool was built for an employer, so the source code and real data aren't published here. The diagram and descriptions are simplified and contain no proprietary mappings or business rules.

=== Louisville Crime: 2024 vs. 2025 ===
Status: Past (Completed), 2025–2026
Project page: https://www.davidcreason.com/projects/louisville-crime/
Links: Explore 2024 vs. 2025: https://www.davidcreason.com/projects/louisville-crime/
Tags: Tableau, Python, Public data, Correlation analysis, Year-over-year
Summary: Two-year analysis of 140,212 reported offenses in Louisville, KY across weather, moon phase, offense categories, geography, holidays, and timing. Hot days drive crime up (r = 0.51 in both years); full moons still don't.

I analyzed two full years of Louisville Metro Police Department (LMPD) incident reports (69,876 offenses occurring in 2024 and 70,336 in 2025) alongside daily NOAA weather observations and lunar phases to see which crime patterns hold steady year over year and where 2025 shifted.

- 140,212 reported offenses analyzed (69,876 in 2024 · 70,336 in 2025, +0.7% YoY)

- r = 0.51 daily high-temp correlation in both years (+0.73 to +0.76 offenses per °F)

- -20.1% drop in auto thefts in 2025 (-1,161), alongside homicide (-26.1%) & robbery (-18.4%)

- +28.5% surge in retail shoplifting (+912), with grocery store incidents up +73.6%

- 88 offenses on Jan 6, 2025 (winter storm & ice) — single lowest day across both years

- -20.2% drop in Downtown (40202) offenses in 2025, while outer-ring ZIPs rose

Core findings: 2024 vs. 2025

- Temperature vs. rain (consistent) Daily high temperature showed a nearly identical positive correlation in both years: r = 0.514 in 2024 (+0.73 offenses/°F) and r = 0.511 in 2025 (+0.76 offenses/°F). Rain alone barely moved daily totals (-0.8% on rainy days in 2024; -4.7% in 2025).

- Lunar influence (consistent) Full moons still show no positive effect. The 12 full-moon days averaged 193.7/day vs. 190.8/day in 2024 (+1.5%), and 181.8/day vs. 193.1/day in 2025 (-5.9%).

- Seasonality (consistent) Q3 (Jul–Sep) led both years (18,953 in 2024; 19,245 in 2025), with July as the peak month (6,534 and 6,631) and February as the lowest (5,094 and 4,814).

- Geography (core held, edges shifted) West and central ZIPs 40203 (4,933 → 4,929) and 40211 (4,772 → 4,889) stayed #1 and #2, while Downtown 40202 fell 20.2% (4,078 → 3,256) and southwest/east areas like 40272 (+11.5%), 40216 (+7.1%), and 8th Division (+12.1%) rose.

- Offense mix (diverged sharply) Total volume was flat (+0.7%), but auto theft plunged 20.1% (5,769 → 4,608), homicide fell 26.1% (153 → 113), robbery fell 18.4% (971 → 792), and burglary fell 11.5% (3,517 → 3,113) — offset by surges in shoplifting (+28.5%), narcotics (+21.9%), and trespassing (+39.9%).

- Venue shifts (parking down, retail up) Parking lot and garage offenses fell 11.9% (7,900 → 6,963) alongside the auto-theft drop, while grocery/supermarket incidents jumped 73.6% (818 → 1,420) and department/discount stores rose 19.6% (1,941 → 2,322).

- Day of week (shifted to weekend) Mondays led 2024 (10,519 offenses), ahead of Saturday (10,376). In 2025, activity shifted to the weekend: Saturday (#1 at 10,446) and Friday (#2 at 10,398) overtook Monday (#4 at 9,984).

- Holidays & severe weather (new low) Thanksgiving was the lowest day of 2024 (112) and dropped to 150 in 2025 (Christmas fell to 133). The quietest day across both years was Jan 6, 2025 (88 offenses) during a 7+ inch snow and ice storm.

- Peak days & 1st-of-month effect July 22 topped 2024 (269). In 2025, July 1 (276), Aug 1 (274), and June 1 (273) led raw counts, followed by May 30 (272) — driven by retroactive reports that default to the 1st of the month (13.0% reported >7 days later vs. 3.9% on other days).

What held steady across both years

- Heat drives crime up linearly; rain barely matters: Daily maximum temperature explained ~26% of daily variance in both years (r = 0.514 and r = 0.511), adding roughly 3 more offenses for every +4°F warming, while rainy days (≥0.1″) moved daily totals by less than 5%.

- Full moons don’t move the needle: Across 24 full moons in 2024–2025, full-moon days averaged 187.7 offenses/day versus 191.9/day on all other days.

- Summer Q3 peak, winter Q1 trough: July and August were the #1 and #2 months in both years, while February was the lowest month in both years.

- Geographic concentration: The top four ZIP codes ( 40203 , 40211 , 40214 , 40219 ) held the exact same 1–4 rank order in both 2024 and 2025, accounting for 27% of all citywide offenses.

- Retroactive 1st-of-the-month reporting lag: In both years, the 1st of the month averaged 16–26% more offenses than days 2–31 because delayed reports (13.0% reported >7 days later in 2025 vs. 3.9% on other days) frequently default to the 1st.

What shifted in 2025

- Auto theft, homicide, robbery, and burglary dropped sharply: Auto theft fell 20.1% (-1,161), homicide fell 26.1% (-40), robbery fell 18.4% (-179), and burglary fell 11.5% (-404), while retail shoplifting (+28.5%), narcotics (+21.9%), and trespassing (+39.9%) surged.

- Venue mix moved from parking lots to retail aisles: Parking lot and garage offenses dropped 11.9% (-937), while grocery/supermarket incidents rose 73.6% (+602) and department/discount stores rose 19.6% (+381).

- Downtown dropped while outer-ring ZIPs grew: Downtown ( 40202 ) fell by 822 offenses (-20.2%), while Valley Station ( 40272 , +11.5%), Shively ( 40216 , +7.1%), and LMPD’s 8th Division (+12.1%) saw higher volume.

- Weekend peak replaced the Monday peak: Saturday and Friday became the busiest days of the week in 2025, while Monday fell from #1 to #4 (-5.1%).

- Severe winter weather beat holidays for the quietest day: While Thanksgiving (150) and Christmas (133) stayed quiet in 2025, the January 6, 2025 snow and ice storm brought citywide reports down to just 88 offenses.

=== ITIL Service Management Assessments ===
Status: Past (Graduate coursework), 2011
Project page: https://www.davidcreason.com/projects/itil-service-management/
Links: Read more: https://www.davidcreason.com/projects/itil-service-management/
Tags: ITIL service lifecycle, Service level management, IT service continuity, Supplier management
Summary: For my M.S. ITIL course, I assessed two fictional companies, a global resort operator and a mobile music service, and wrote recommendations to IT leadership on service portfolios, SLAs, supplier contracts, continuity, and change management.

In my M.S. course on IT Planning & Service Management Using ITIL, each assignment put me inside a fictional company with real-world IT problems and asked for recommendations to leadership. The companies were invented for the course; the analysis and recommendations below are mine.

Resort operator: an ITIL roadmap for the VP of IT

A global resort company with no defined list of IT services, weak service levels, and aging systems nobody had retired.

Service portfolio

- Defined the portfolio: a pipeline, a catalog of 12 live services (from the service desk to reservations, lodging, ski, golf, and club management), and a retired list.

- Showed how never retiring services had left legacy systems in place.

Service levels & suppliers

- Replaced unmeasurable SLAs with the full set: service level requirements, measurable SLAs, internal OLAs, and a service improvement plan.

- Specified OLAs with every department, Finance, and HR.

- Identified six outside suppliers that needed underpinning contracts, including 24/7 support for the business-critical website.

Continuity

- Assigned every service a recovery tier based on what an outage would cost the business (below).

| | Tier | Services | Why

| Immediate recovery | Reservations, Website, Lodging, Customer relationship management, Club management | Revenue stops the moment these go down. Reservations and the website serve guests across time zones around the clock, and club members are the most loyal customers.
| Fast recovery | Financial management, Material management | Used by nearly every division, but guests can still check in and stay while they're down. Purchasing still matters: guests notice when the restaurant runs out of something.
| Gradual recovery | Human resources | Not critical to daily operations, with paper records as a fallback. Restoring from backup is acceptable, and faster options would cost more than the downtime.
| Manual workaround | Ski management, Golf management | Each resort can take bookings by phone and in a daily planner until the system returns, the way it worked before computers.

Mobile music service: fix the foundation before expanding

A fast-growing company weighing two new lines of business, a mobile game and a dating app, after a 48-hour outage.

Recommendation

- Advised leadership to hold off on both launches until core IT service management was in place.

- In an earlier memo to leadership, explained what ITSM is and why it would align IT with the business: utility and warranty, clear ownership, and accountability.

Top three risks

- Availability: no formal service levels, and an implied 99.999% target already missed.

- People and legacy tech: a COBOL finance and procurement system, a shrinking talent pool, and falling morale.

- Change management: each office configured differently, no release management, and no known-error database.

Why it still matters

- It's the same judgment I use today: tie IT decisions to business impact, standardize before you scale, and put controls around change.
